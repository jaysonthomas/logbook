Simplification of rotation derivatives

Imagine a robot's end-effector frame rotating in space. This frame is represented by a rotation matrix $R$, where its columns are the unit vectors of its axes: $R = \begin{bmatrix} x & y & z \end{bmatrix}$.

As the frame spins with an angular velocity $\omega$, those axes change over time. The time derivative of the rotation matrix ($\dot{R}$) gives an idea of how fast $x$, $y$, and $z$ axes are moving at the instant?

From basic physics, the velocity of the tip of each axis vector is given by the cross product: $$\begin{align*} \dot{x} &= \omega \times x = [\omega]x \\ \dot{y} &= \omega \times y = [\omega]y \\ \dot{z} &= \omega \times z = [\omega]z $$ Putting these columns back into a single matrix equation: $$ \dot{R} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot{x} & \dot{y} & \dot{z} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} [\omega]x & [\omega]y & [\omega]z \end{bmatrix} $$ Factoring out $[\omega]$ gives the fundamental differential equation of rotational kinematics: $$\dot{R} = [\omega]R$$ The matrix $[\omega]$ acts as a velocity operator. $R$ tells where the frame's axes are currently pointing. $[\omega]$ acts on $R$ to tell how fast and in what direction those axes are currently pivoting. Without unrolling $\omega$ into the matrix $[\omega]$, it wouldn't be possible to write the elegant differential relationship $\dot{R} = [\omega]R$, which is the foundation for tracking orientations in flight dynamics, computer graphics, and robotics control loop state estimation.

getTowerPeakIndices

One pass over the array: O(n).

Since peaks cannot be adjacent, it produces tpIndex of size $P \leq \frac{n}{2}$ where $n$ is the number of towers.

getTrappedWaterBetweenPeaks

It sums heights between two given peak indices. Across all calls in trappedWater, the ranges it scans are contiguous and non-overlapping. Summed over the whole run this is only O(n) total.

getPIndexOfNextHighestPeak

This is the expensive part. It scans forward through tpIndex until it finds a peak at least as high as the current one, tracking the highest seen so far as a fallback. In the best case it returns on the first comparison (O(1)) if peaks are increasing.

Consider a zig-zag input with strictly decreasing peak heights, e.g.: $$ 100, 1, 99, 1, 98, 1, 97, 1, ... $$ $P \approx \frac{n}{2}$ peaks exist. The outer loop in TrappedWater advances by only 1 peak per iteration, which leads to: $$ O(P) + O(P-1) + O(P-2) + ... + O(1) $$ This is the sum of $P$ positive integers in the reverse order: $$ P + (P-1) + (P-2) + \cdot + 1 = \sum^P_{k=1} k = \frac{P(P+1)}{2} = \frac{P^2 + P}{2} $$ Since the dominant term is $P^2$, the worst-case scan pattern degrades the algorithm to quadratic time $O(P^2)$.

Since $P = \Theta (n)$, the worst case time is $O(n^2)$.

Big theta

The big theta notation denotes a tight bound (both upper and lower bound). It means $P$ grows at exactly the rate of $n$, not just at most or at least that rate.

Sufficient condition

Overview

The Hessian $\frac{\partial}{\partial{x}} \left(\nabla{f(x)}\right)\bigg|_{x^*}$ can be 0 at a max, min or a saddle point.

The interest is in the min case alone. At a minimum, the second order sufficient condition (a condition on the Hessian of the objective function) dictates that the curve needs to be both flat and curved up around it.

Scalar case

Considering the scalar case of a Newton step - the division will only consist of scalars, i.e. the Jacobian and Hessian are scalars. $$ \Delta{x} = - \left(\nabla^2 f(x)\right)^{-1} \nabla{f(x)} $$
$$\begin{array}{ccl} - &:& \text{Negative sign implies negative descent along the objective function.} \\ \nabla{f(x)} &:& \text{The objective function. Gradient.} \\ \nabla^2 f(x) &:& \text{The Hessian.} \end{array}$$
The Hessian is equivalent to a learning rate or step size. In order to not go uphill, the Hessian needs to be a positive learning rate. $$\begin{align*} \nabla^2 f > 0 &\quad \text{Leads to descent or minimisation} \\ \nabla^2 f < 0 &\quad \text{Leads to ascent or maximisation} \\ \end{align*}$$

Higher dimensional vector case

For minimisation, the Hessian needs to be strictly positive definite, $\nabla^2 f > 0$ (all eigenvalues are positive). Another way of writing it: $$ \nabla^2 f \in S^n_{++} $$
$$\begin{array}{ccl} S^n &:& \text{Symmetric matrix in n dimensions.} \\ ++ &:& \text{Strictly positive definite} \end{array}$$
If it were a single $+$, it would mean semi-positive definite meaning $\nabla^2 f \geq 0$.

In $\mathbb{R}^n$, by choosing the right coordinates (related to eigenvectors/values?), the Hessian matrix can be diagonalised, which decouples the objective function into $n$ scalar systems.

Convergence guarantees

Strictly positive definite hessian

If the Hessian of an objective function is globally positive definite, everywhere in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the function is said to be strongly convex. It's a big bowl. In which case, Newton's method is guaranteed to converge to the global optimum. $$ \nabla^2 f > 0 \, \rightarrow \, f(x)\text{ is strongly convex} \\ $$ In the strictly positive definite case, it's the global optimum.

When the objective function is strongly convex, guarantees can be provided of the runtime of Newton's method - predictions can be made of its runtime, how much sample rate is possible, all of which is important for real time control.

Positive sem-definite hessian

Weakly convex $\nabla^2 f \geq 0$ means that there are flat spots in the bowl. In the semi-definite case, it is possible to have a flat bottom like a valley where all the points are equivalent. The optimal solution found could be one of many global optimum.

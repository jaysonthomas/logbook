Process noise

Overview

Kalman filter converts all uncertainty into an additive noise term. This keeps the estimation problem tractable. $$ x_k = Ax_{k-1} + Bu_k + w_k $$
$$\begin{array}{ccl} A &:& \text{Propagates the state dynamics} \\ B &:& \text{Maps inputs/disturbances into the state} \end{array}$$
The covariance gets bigger after prediction because some process noise or transition covariance $W$ or $Q$ is added.

The covariance matrix $W$ captures the uncertainty associated with state predictions due to a system's inability to precisely execute a given command (from the previous time step to get to the current time step).

Dynamics uncertainty

If the true system is: $$ x_k = (A + \Delta A)x_{k-1} + Bu_k $$ The effective process noise is: $$ w_k = \Delta Ax_{k-1} $$ The effective covariance becomes state dependent: $$ Cov(w_k) = Cov(\Delta Ax_{k-1}) $$ This is harder to handle since it is multiplicative noise, not simple additive white noise - the uncertainty grows with state magnitude which breaks the assumptions of the ordinary KF.

Input uncertainty

If uncertainty is modeled as noise on the input/control channel $$\begin{align*} x_k &= A x_{k-1} + B(u_k + \eta_k) \\ \eta_k &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_\eta) \\ w_k &= B\eta_k \end{align*}$$ The process covariance becomes: $$\begin{align*} W &= E[w_k w_k^\top] \\ &= E[(B\eta_k)(B\eta_k)^\top] \\ &= B\Sigma_\eta B^\top \end{align*}$$
$$\begin{array}{ccl} W \text{or } Q &:& \text{Process noise covariance matrix} \\ && \text{Determined experimentally based on the system's model. Usually remains constant.} \\ \end{array}$$
$B$ in the covariance suggests that the process uncertainty enters the system through the same channels as the control/disturbance input channel.

Examples

  • Imperfect actuators or motor torque uncertainty
  • Unknown external force (could be because of model imperfections) entering through the same physics as the actuator
  • Discretization uncertainty in the input

Forgetting factor

High process noise is similar to a forgetting factor. It down-weights predictions (lower trust in the propagation) and up-weights newer measurements.

Example

$$ W = B \; 0.1 \; B^\top + 10^{-5} I $$ The small diagonal term adds a tiny isotropic background uncertainty everywhere. It is usually added to ensure:
  • positive definiteness
  • numerical stability
  • full rank covariance
Without it, $B\Sigma_\eta B^\top$ can be singular if B has low rank.

Practicalities

Choose the process variance to be large enough to absorb unmodeled dynamics, linearisation error, parameter drift, disturbances etc.

A standard Kalman filter assumes: $$ w_k \sim \mathbb N(0, W) $$ with covariance independent of the state.

Uncertainty is injected into the state evolution in a controlled way to compensate for any surprises.

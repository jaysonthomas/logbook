Kalman Gain intuition

The Core Scalar Analogy: A Ratio of UncertaintiesIf this were a 1-dimensional (scalar) problem where state and measurements are directly comparable, the Kalman Gain formula simplifies to a familiar ratio:$$L_k = \frac{\text{Uncertainty in Prediction}}{\text{Total Uncertainty in Measurement space}} = \frac{\Sigma_{\text{prediction}}}{\Sigma_{\text{prediction}} + R}$$If Prediction Uncertainty ($\Sigma_{k\vert{}k-1}$) is huge: $L_k \approx 1$. You don't trust your prediction, so you rely almost entirely on the new sensor measurement.If Sensor Noise ($R$) is huge: $S_k$ is very large, making $L_k \approx 0$. You don't trust the sensor, so you ignore the new measurement and stick with your physical prediction.Now let's examine each term in the matrix equation to see how this works in multiple dimensions.2. Deconstructing Each Matrix TermA. $\Sigma_{k\vert{}k-1}$ — The Predicted State CovarianceRole: Represents the filter's prior uncertainty about the physical state right before seeing the new measurement.Intuition: This acts as the numerator of confidence weighting. If $\Sigma_{k\vert{}k-1}$ is large along a particular direction in state space (e.g., high uncertainty in velocity), it signals: "We are very unsure about this state dimension, so we are open to making a large correction to it if a measurement arrives."B. $C$ and $C^\top$ — The Measurement Matrix & Dimension BridgeRole: $C$ maps states (e.g., $[position, velocity]^\top$) into measurement space (e.g., measuring only position). $C^\top$ performs the reverse mapping.Intuition: 1. Unit and Dimensional Alignment: State space and measurement space often have different dimensions and units. $C^\top$ converts the inverse measurement covariance back into the coordinate frame and units of the state estimate.2. Cross-Correlation Bridge: Even if a sensor only measures position directly ($C = [1, 0]$), the multiplication $\Sigma_{k\vert{}k-1} C^\top$ extracts how position uncertainty correlates with velocity uncertainty. This allows the Kalman filter to update velocity even when reading a position sensor!C. $S_k^{-1}$ — The Inverse Innovation CovarianceRole: $S_k = C \Sigma_{k\vert{}k-1} C^\top + R_k$ is the total expected uncertainty in the measurement space (combining projected prediction uncertainty and sensor noise $R_k$).Intuition: Taking the inverse ($S_k^{-1}$) turns uncertainty into precision / trustworthiness (since Precision = $\frac{1}{\text{Variance}}$).$S_k^{-1}$ acts as the denominator of the gain ratio.If $S_k$ is large (due to high sensor noise $R_k$ or noisy predictions), $S_k^{-1}$ becomes small, reducing the overall Kalman Gain $L_k$.3. Putting It All Together: The Three Roles in ActionWhen you multiply them together:$$L_k = \underbrace{\Sigma_{k\vert{}k-1} C^\top}_{\substack{\text{Uncertainty in prediction} \\ \text{mapped to cross-space correlation}}} \quad \times \quad \underbrace{S_k^{-1}}_{\substack{\text{Inverse total measurement uncertainty} \\ \text{(Sensor Precision)}}}$$$S_k^{-1}$ evaluates sensor reliability: It checks how noisy the sensor domain is. High noise $\rightarrow$ small $S_k^{-1}$.$\Sigma_{k\vert{}k-1}$ checks model reliability: It checks how uncertain the state prediction is. High prediction uncertainty $\rightarrow$ large $\Sigma_{k\vert{}k-1}$.$C^\top$ maps the correction: It ensures that the correction vector is correctly scaled, rotated, and applied to all correlated state variables.

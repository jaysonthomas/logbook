Reasons for log

Curved manifold

Because $SE(3)$ is a curved manifold, it is not possible to simply subtract two transformation matrices ($X_d - X$) to find the error. It wouldn't yield a valid transformation matrix, nor a meaningful velocity vector. Refer lie space. $$ [\mathcal{V}_{ed}] = \log(T_{ed}) = \begin{bmatrix} [\omega_{ed}] & v_{ed} \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

No time scaling

The error twist represents an instantaneous pose error displacement. It gives the direction and distance (displacement) required to warp the current frame $X$ onto the desired frame $X_d$.

In a standard kinematic task-space controller, it is used in the following way: $$V_{\text{command}} = V_{ff} + K_p V_{ed}$$

$$\begin{array}{ccl} V_{ff} &:& \text{Feedforward velocity already scaled by } \frac{1}{\Delta t} \\ K_p &:& \text{Proportional feedback gain matrix. Inherently carries the units of frequency } \frac{1}{\text{s}} \end{array}$$
$K_p$ handles the time-scaling component, turning the error displacement into a corrective velocity command. The feedforward term divides by $\Delta t$ because it needs to convert a scheduled finite displacement into an immediate velocity.

Nature of $K_p$

The error term could be scaled by $1/\Delta t$. However, doing so changes the physical meaning of $K_p$ and impacts how the system handles tuning and stability. The second term would become: $$K'_p \frac{V_{ed}}{\Delta t}$$ Instead of $K_p$ acting as a frequency-based multiplier ($1/\text{s}$) that determines how quickly the system aggressively eliminates an error, the new gain ($K'_p$) becomes a dimensionless scaling factor.

Deadbeat Control

Setting $K'_p = 1$, would mean that the controller would calculate the exact velocity needed to completely eliminate any tracking error in exactly one control cycle ($\Delta t$). In control theory, this is known as deadbeat control. This introduces a few issues in physical systems:
  • Extreme Actuator Saturation
    • Physical robots have real limits on torque, current, and velocity. If the robot experiences a sudden disturbance (e.g., you push it, or it encounters an obstacle), $V_{ed}$ instantly becomes large. Dividing a large error by a tiny control period (like $\Delta t = 0.001\text{ s}$ for a $1\text{ kHz}$ loop) results in a massive, spike-like command that will instantly saturate your motors or cause the system to fault.

  • Sensitivity to Noise and Jitter
    • Real-world sensor data is noisy. If your loop rate varies slightly (hardware jitter) or your state estimation has high-frequency noise, dividing that noise by a microscopic $\Delta t$ dramatically amplifies it, leading to shaky, high-frequency vibrations in the joints.

  • Coupling Gain to Loop Frequency
    • Keeping $\Delta t$ inside the gain means that if you decide to upgrade your controller hardware to run at a faster loop rate (say, moving from $500\text{ Hz}$ to $1\text{ kHz}$), your controller's physical responsiveness won't radically distort overnight. The feedback loop remains governed by a time-constant independent of how fast the computer can spin its while-loop.
SummaryThe feedforward term must use $\Delta t$ because it tracks a pre-planned, deterministic trajectory over a known time slice. The feedback term drops $\Delta t$ to act as a smooth, programmable "spring" ($K_p$) that coaxes the robot back on track without causing explosive velocity commands

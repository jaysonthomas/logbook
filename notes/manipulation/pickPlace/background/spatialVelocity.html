Spatial Velocity

Overview

Also called end effector twist. It is the derivative of a pose. $$V^G = \begin{bmatrix} \omega \\ v \end{bmatrix}$$
$$\begin{array}{ccl} V^G &:& \text{Spatial velocity of a gripper} \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 1} \\ \omega &:& \text{Angular velocities} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 1} \\ v &:& \text{Translational velocities} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 1} \end{array}$$
The angular velocity vector represents the angular rate (magnitude) and around what (instantaneous) axis the end-effector is rotating. The linear velocity vector represents how fast the end-effector's origin is moving.

There is no standard/canonical choice of representation for a pose. Based on a chosen representation, an orientation (which is part of a pose), can have various problems hence the need for constantly moving between representations. However there is a canonical choice for angular velocity, consisting of 3 numbers representing the rotation rates around the major axis.

For orientation, problems arise because angles wrap around. Whereas, angular velocities do not; a higher value means higher speed.

Unit quaternions live on the surface of a 3D sphere. Angular velocities live on a tangent plane to a particular point on the sphere.

Relationship to joint velocities

$$ V^G = J^G(q) \, v $$
$$\begin{array}{ccl} q &:& \text{Joint positions} \\ v &:& \text{Joint velocities} \\ J(q) &:& \text{Geometric Jacobian; changes based on current configuration of joints.} \\ \end{array}$$
It's a non-trivial relationship to joint positions. But, once the instantaneous joint positions are known, the derivative/jacobian provides a simple linear mapping from joint velocities to spatial velocity where in small changes in joint velocities cause equivalent changes in the gripper velocity.

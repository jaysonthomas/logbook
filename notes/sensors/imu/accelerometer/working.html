Working principle

Overview

According to Newton's 2nd law: $$ a = \frac{1}{m}F $$
$$\begin{array}{ccl} F &:& \text{Sum of all forces acting on an object} \\ a &:& \text{Total acceleration} \end{array}$$
Accelerometers don't measure total acceleration, but specific acceleration which is the acceleration caused by all the forces acting on a object except for gravity.

The output of an accelerometer is actually: $$ a_{accel} = \frac{1}{m}(F - F_g) $$

A full 3-axis accelerometer will have 3 of these in orthogonal directions.

Hardware setup

A simple accelerometer consists of a proof mass attached inside of some housing that is constrained to only move vertically. The proof mass is attached between the top and bottom of the housing with 2 springs. It is calibrated to be at the centre when the net external vertical force acting on the housing is $0$. The springs stretch/contract to keep the proof mass in the centre.
An accelerometer measures the difference between the upwards force provided by the upper spring and the downwards force provided by the lower spring. $$ F_{springs,net} = F_{s,up} - F_{s,down} $$

Example measurements

Resting on an object

When an accelerometer is sitting on a table, the housing and the proof mass have $0$ acceleration. To keep the proof mass in the centre, the net force from the springs is upwards and equal to $mg$. The accelerometer would measure a net acceleration upwards that's equal to $g$.

Free fall

If the sensor were in free fall, the housing and the proof mass would accelerate downwards at $9.8 m/s^2$. To keep the proof mass centered in the housing, the two springs would be pulling equally. The net force from the springs would be $0$. The accelerometer would measure $0$ acceleration.
